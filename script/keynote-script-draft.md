# Keynote script.

## Section 1.
In 1842 Edwin Chadwick published his Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population, and in it was a table of the average age at death, by class and by town. Manchester: gentry and professional men, 38. Tradesmen, 20. Labourers, 17. In Rutland, then a county of fields, a labourer died at 38. So a farm hand in the Midlands could expect the life of a Manchester gentleman.

The table was wrong, as it happens. William Farr pointed out within months that average age at death, computed from the dead alone, is not life expectancy. A town full of young migrants and dying infants will always score badly. Chadwick knew. He printed it anyway, because it was the only arithmetic he had, and the Poor Law Committee that had commissioned it disowned the report. But six years later Parliament passed the Public Health Act.

Behind the table was a real place, our place. James Kay, a Manchester physician, had walked cottonopolis in 1832 and written up what he found among the cotton operatives. Friedrich Engels walked it in 1844: in the bare ruined choirs of Little Ireland, in the bend of the Medlock below Oxford Road, where the river ran black and the cellars flooded and families of ten slept in two rooms. Cholera came in 1832 and again in 1849. Typhus was seasonal. Tuberculosis never went anywhere.

In the end the capitalists who had built the mills found it was worth paying for fresh clean water from the Pennines and to put sewers under the streets. The infirmary had already been built by public subscription in 1752. Owens College followed in 1851 on a merchant's bequest. None of this came from a Westminster, happy to benefit from increased trade, but reluctant to reward the undeserving poor. The city's improvement was subscribed by Manchester people.

That line is public health, water and sewers first, then vaccines, then antibiotics, long before the quiet revolution of blood pressure pills, statins, the end of smoking in pubs. Seventeen became forty, forty became sixty, sixty became eighty.

In June 1948 a machine in a room on Coupland Street ran the first program ever stored in electronic memory. Freddie Williams and Tom Kilburn built it. Alan Turing joined them that autumn. And across the campus the Professor of Neurosurgery, a man named Geoffrey Jefferson, who had spent thirty years opening skulls and knew the brain as a wet, bleeding, feeling, particular organ, watched the newspapers call the thing an electronic brain and completely lost his mind.

On the ninth of June 1949 he stood up at the Royal College of Surgeons in London to give the Lister Oration, and called it The Mind of Mechanical Man. His argument came down to one sentence: no machine equals a brain until it can write a sonnet "because of thoughts and emotions felt". The Times reported it the next morning. Their reporter rang Manchester, and Turing, who could never resist, said that a machine's sonnet would probably be better appreciated by another machine. 

That autumn the two of them sat in the same seminars in the philosophy department here, arguing. Jefferson gave Turing an offprint of the lecture. Turing read it with a pencil in his hand, and that offprint, with his marks in the margin, is in a box in Cambridge today. From it he wrote the paper that every one of you has heard of, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, in 1950, and he put Jefferson's sonnet in it by name as the objection he most needed to answer. In January 1952 the BBC put the two of them in a studio together, with Max Newman refereeing, for the first recorded argument about artificial intelligence that exists. Two Fellows of the Royal Society, in one city, arguing for three years about whether the machine down the corridor had a mind. Jefferson died in 1961 and in a postscript the year before he called Turing his most ingenious colleague and said Turing had believed passionately that the machines had all but solved the problem of the mind.
The question Jefferson asked was what a brain is, such that it can feel its way to a sonnet.

Dementia is a disease you have to be old enough to catch. In Chadwick's Manchester almost nobody was. Every year that line climbed, the city delivered more of its people into the one illness that waits at the far end of a long life. Medicine saved the body, and the body lived long enough to lose the person it was carrying.

In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire, That on the ashes of his youth doth lie, As the death-bed whereon it must expire, Consumed with that which it was nourished by.

Jefferson said a machine cannot write a sonnet because it cannot feel. Turing said one day it will. There is a third case, and it was walking past both their windows in 1949: a human brain that wrote sonnets, felt them, and then one year could no longer read them. Neither man put it in the argument.

His GP said what GPs have said for most of medicine's history, because for most of medicine's history it was the only honest thing to say: probably just age, come back if it gets worse. It got worse. By the time he reached a memory clinic the sessions of sweet silent thought were over. He could not summon up remembrance of things past. He could not grieve for his wife, because he was not sure she was dead. 

Thomas De Quincey, born on Cross Street in 1785 and schooled at Manchester Grammar, wrote in Suspiria de Profundis (1845) the passage on the brain as a palimpsest, every experience written over the last and none erased, which is the most quoted nineteenth-century account of memory as an organ, and it was written by a Mancunian about a Manchester childhood.

Five of the 1966 eleven had it. 

In 2018 a group of epidemiologists re-ran Chadwick's table with modern death records for 2010 to 2012, and Manchester was still at the bottom of it, in every occupational class, 170 years on.

So let me tell you what the people in this room have already done about it.

Three miles from here, a nurse called Alex Cook sees a patient who has come in worried about her memory. Before lunch that patient has had a blood test for a protein called pTau217, which, if it is raised, means the disease Alzheimer described in 1906 is already in her brain, years before she will fail a memory test. Nicola Kendall has measured the stiffness of her arteries. Hala Shokr has photographed the vessels at the back of her eye. By the afternoon she has a research-grade MRI and, if she needs it, a lumbar puncture; the clinic has done more than two hundred of those. [satisfaction figure, if cleared] Nearly half of the people who walk through that door walk out with a change to their blood pressure or their statin on the same day.

Across the road, Adam Greenstein's laboratory is working on the small arteries of the brain itself, the ones that silt up in people like Frank.
And a pharmaceutical company has decided to put a thousand-person, seven-year study of memory and blood vessels in this city rather than in Boston or Oxford, because Manchester can recruit the patients, scan them, bleed them and keep them.

What else than a natural and mighty palimpsest is the human brain? Such a palimpsest is my brain; such a palimpsest, O reader! is at school yours”
