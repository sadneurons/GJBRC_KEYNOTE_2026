# Keynote script, 15 minutes

Thanks to Stuart  for asking me present this keynote, the only thing between you and the vino. I'll try to keep your minds off the booze for the next 25 minutes, and I'm happy to bore you with the detail afterwards.

I'm going to talk about this city, this University, and their history, and then I'll talk about what the Brain Health Centre is and why it represents a crossroads in the development of our approach to the diseases underlying dementia. 

We'll follow one woman, Maisie, born 77 years ago at home in Harpurhey on Thursday the ninth of June 1949, in a two-up two-down off Rochdale Road, the third of five. Her father works at the gasworks. Mam works at home until the youngest is at school and then takes up a cleaning job at the local school.

[sonnet slide]

The afternoon she's born, in London, Geoffrey Jefferson, Professor of Neurosurgery in this university, a man who had spent thirty years with his hands on living brains, stood up at the Royal College of Surgeons to give the Lister Oration. He called it the *Mind of Mechanical Man*. It was about a machine on Coupland Street, a year older than the baby in Harpurhey, which the newspapers had taken to calling an electronic brain. Jefferson had lost patience with the phrase. "No machine", he said, "could be called a brain until it had written a sonnet or a concerto because of thoughts and emotions felt, and knew that it had written it."

The Times reported it the next morning. Their correspondent telephoned Manchester, and Alan Turing, who had come here the previous autumn to programme the machine, said a sonnet written by a machine would probably be better appreciated by another machine.

That ran on the Saturday under the heading "Calculus to Sonnet", and this challenge, the question of whether a machine has a mind, became the basis for his 1950 paper COMPUTING MACHINERY AND INTELLIGENCE. In May 1951 the BBC puts Jefferson and Turing in a studio to argue about whether machines can think. The recording is lost, but the transcript's here if you want to read it.

## 2. The three women before her (2:00 to 4:00)

Slide: Chadwick's 1842 table, as printed.

Her great-grandmother was born in Manchester too, in 1840, in the bare ruined choirs of Little Ireland, whose cellars near the bend of the Medlock at the bottom of Oxford Road  Friedrich Engels walked through four years later and could not get the smell of out of his prose. In 1842 Edwin Chadwick printed a table: average age at death, by class and by town. Manchester labourers, seventeen. Manchester gentry, thirty-eight. Labourers in Rutland, a county of fields, thirty-eight. Her great-grandmother died of typhus at twenty nine, leaving 4 surviving children of an original 9.

The Public Health Act passed in 1848. The water came down from Longdendale. The sewers went under the streets. The cellars were condemned. The infirmary was built by subscription in 1752, this University by a merchant's will in 1851.

Her grandmother, born in the same district in 1878, lived to fifty-six. Her own mother, born in 1915, will reach sixty-one. Three women from one square mile, and an increasingly long life is built a generation at a time in wave after wave of slum clearances and social housing, cleaner water and cleaner air, and, of course, the establishment of the NHS, still this country's single greatest social and democratic achievement. Inoculation, health checks in schools, fortified flour and fluoridated water.  By the time Maisie is born a Manchester girl can expect about seventy years, and for the first time in that family's long history, this is going to be old enough to reach the age at which brains begin to fail. The NHS saved the machine, through nutrition, antibiotics, heart transplants, ... and ... .and now the machine lasts long enough to outlive the mind.

## 3. The first forks

When Maisie is two,

In 1960, she sits the eleven-plus, and her life divides for the first time. In one life she is one of the 30% who pass and takes the bus to the grammar school. In another she walks to the secondary modern. That winter Jefferson dies at seventy-four.

At fifteen, on both branches, she learns a sonnet by heart for the O-level. Hers is the seventy-third. "That time of year thou mayst in me behold". She is fifteen and has no idea what it is about. But she can say all fourteen lines in forty seconds without taking a breath.

At sixteen, our second fork. In one life, she stays on at school. The family is small enough that another income isn't needed just yet, and dad is still able to work. In another, she gets a job with Ferranti in Moston, on the assembly line, soldering the components that will go to make wirelesses and televisions, and the Mercury, the commercial version of the machine Jefferson and Turing argued over. At the bus stop on Moston Lane she coughs over her first cigarette, and thinks she looks like Brigitte Bardot.

From here the branches multiply faster than I can follow, so I will keep to four of her, and tell you at each fork what happened

Blood pressure. In 1999, at fifty, one of our Maisies goes to the GP about her knee and has her blood pressure taken while she is there. It is 165 over 95. She leaves with a prescription and a nurse who rings her to make sure things are okay on the new medication. The other version of her has the same systolic and no knee pain, and so it sits untreated for fifteen years, doing to the small arteries of her brain what Adam's laboratory across the road now watches happen under a microscope.

Smoking. One of her stopped at forty, when her husband was told he had angina and the two of them stopped together. One never did. 

Hearing. At sixty, this one of her gets hearing aids, because her daughter has a professional job and can take a morning off to sit with her at Crumpsall. The other stops going to the Tuesday club at the Church because she cannot follow the conversation through the chatter, then stops going out, and spends a decade in an otherwise quiet house with just the television gradually increasing in volume.

In the spring of 2026, at seventy-seven, she tells the story about the Ferranti foreman twice in one afternoon, and her daughter books her a GP appointment.

One GP, in a twelve-minute slot, says what GPs have said for most of medicine's history, because for most of that history it was the only honest thing to say: probably just age, come back if it gets worse. It gets worse. She comes back in 2029 and fails a memory test that confirms what we could have told her three years earlier. Clinical Dementia Rating 2. Moderate dementia. Not eligible for any trial.

The other GP takes the repeated story seriously and refers her that day to the Memory clinic.

Nicola Kendall has measured the stiffness of her arteries and found her blood pressure. Hala Shokr has photographed the vessels at the back of her eye. By the afternoon she has had a research-grade MRI and, if she needs one, a lumbar puncture; the clinic has done more than two hundred of those. Nobody can promise her anything. But something has happened to her that never happened to anyone before her in that house off Rochdale Road. For four generations nobody lived long enough for their brains to betray them. Hers is the first to be looked at, measured, and reported back to its owner as something to take care of. Jefferson's condition for a mind was that it should know what it had done. She is the first to liev long enough for her mind to become an issue for her, and so we owe it to her to help her understand why it's failing.

## 6. Four women, seventy-seven (12:00 to 13:30)

So here are four women. All of them are her, all of them are alive in Manchester tonight, and all of them are seventy-seven.

The first was seen nine years ago, at sixty-eight and told she had Mild cognitive impairment. She lives in a home now in Blackley. Few visitors. When to the sessions of sweet silent thought she tries to summon up remembrance of things past, nothing comes; she cannot grieve for her husband because she is not sure that he is dead. She can still sing, and the staff use Beatles songs to get her into the bath.

The second one was told she has Alzheimer's disease, early enough she could make plans, tell the grandkids all the Ferranti stories, the one whose GP referred her in time and landed in the right place. She knows what her brain is doing.

The third is well. She walks to the shops, argues with the radio, and will be diagnosed at eighty-four.

The fourth will say the whole of Sonnet 73 at her great-grandson's wedding in 2040, at ninety-one, standing, and will die at ninety-three without once being unable to find her husband's name.

The NHS was a fork in the road for the whole country. Our aim is to make the brain health centre do the same, to change how we think about ageing, the diseases that affect.

## 7. Which branch (13:30 to 15:00)

Which of the four she becomes was decided, mostly, before she could vote. The rate at which people of a given age develop dementia has been falling in England since the 1990s, which nobody planned and no drug did; and when the cohorts are split by schooling or income, the fall is in the top third and nowhere else. In 2018 epidemiologists re-ran Chadwick's table with modern death records, and Manchester was still at the bottom, in every class, a hundred and seventy years on. The gradient did not go anywhere. It is now a gradient in the age at which the sonnet goes. The fourth woman mostly lives in Didsbury. The first mostly lives in Harpurhey, where she started.
